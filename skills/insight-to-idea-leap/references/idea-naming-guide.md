# Idea Naming Guide

How to name a brand idea — the internal shorthand that makes ideas transmissible, memorable, and easy to brief from.

---

## What the Name Does

The idea name is not the tagline. It is not what the public sees. It is the internal compass — the two to five words that everyone in the room uses when they mean the idea. It should be:

- **Short enough to say in a sentence** without slowing down
- **Evocative enough** that hearing it produces the idea, not just the words
- **Unusual enough** that it couldn't belong to a different idea in a different category

The test: say the name to someone who wasn't in the room. Do they ask "what does that mean?" (good — the name is doing work) or do they nod politely (bad — it's too generic to land).

---

## The Four Naming Approaches

### 1. The Named Tension
Name the contradiction inside the insight. The name holds both sides.

*Examples:*
- "Proud Humility" (for a brand that celebrates achievement without arrogance)
- "Private Ambition" (for a brand that acknowledges the gap between public and private self)
- "The Visible Invisible" (for a brand that makes overlooked people seen)
- "Moving Room" (for a car brand that is really about personal sanctuary)

**When to use:** When the insight is fundamentally about a contradiction that the brand doesn't resolve but inhabits.

---

### 2. The Stolen Object
Name something from a completely different domain that captures the idea's essence. The unexpectedness of the object is the naming strategy.

*Examples:*
- "The Keeper of Recipes" (food brand positioned around culinary memory)
- "The Unchallengeable Choice" (fleet brand for decision-maker confidence)
- "The Informed Partnership" (hospital brand treating patients as collaborators)
- "The Second Wardrobe" (fashion brand for the private style that's going public)

**When to use:** When the idea has been reached through the Stolen Frame reframing move, or when a concrete object from outside the category captures the brand's function better than category language can.

---

### 3. The Verb
Name what the brand does — but in an unexpected way. Not "we connect" or "we empower" — a verb that is specific enough to be unusual.

*Examples:*
- "We Introduce" (loyalty brand built on the act of sharing something valuable)
- "We Remember" (brand built around knowing the customer as a whole person)
- "We Hold" (a brand that exists in moments of transition or uncertainty)
- "We Refuse" (a brand built on category refusal)

**When to use:** When the brand's differentiating action is the idea, and naming the action is more powerful than naming what it produces.

---

### 4. The Unexpected Adjective
A single adjective that reframes what the brand is. The adjective is not the expected one for the category.

*Examples:*
- "The Quiet Ambition" (aspiration brand that doesn't shout)
- "The Earned Brand" (premium brand whose premium feels deserved, not claimed)
- "The Patient Brand" (a brand that plays a long game with its audience)
- "The Permission Brand" (a brand that gives the audience license they didn't know they needed)

**When to use:** When the idea is primarily about *how* the brand operates or feels, rather than *what* it does.

---

## Naming Anti-Patterns

**The Category Noun**: Using a word that belongs to the category, not the brand. "The Connection Brand." "The Wellness Brand." "The Innovation Platform." These names could belong to anyone. They have no edge.

**The Value Statement**: Naming a value instead of an idea. "Authenticity." "Trust." "Courage." Values are ingredients of ideas, not ideas themselves. They're too broad to generate executions.

**The Double Adjective**: Two adjectives that feel safe together. "Bold Simplicity." "Warm Confidence." "Modern Heritage." These feel strategic but communicate nothing specific. The pairing usually means neither word is doing enough work.

**The Gerund Trap**: Names that end in "-ing" and feel like a process, not a position. "Connecting Communities." "Building Futures." "Enabling Growth." They describe activity without claiming territory.

---

## Testing the Name

Ask three questions:

1. **Could it be another brand's name?** If a competitor could use this name without it feeling wrong, it's not specific enough.

2. **Does it produce the idea when said alone?** Say the name without any explanation. Does it carry the idea, or does it need three sentences of context to make sense? Strong names carry weight; weak names need scaffolding.

3. **Does a creative team know what to do with it?** Read the name aloud to a creative director. Do they immediately have an image, a tone, a direction? Or do they look at you blankly? The name should unlock executions, not close them down.
