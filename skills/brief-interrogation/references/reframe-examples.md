# Reframe Examples — Brief Interrogation in Action

Five worked examples showing original brief → interrogation findings → reframed brief. Each demonstrates how the eight questions surface a sharper, more useful strategic starting point.

---

## Example 1 — Regional Flour Brand Entering a Neighbouring Market

**Original Request:**
"Develop a 360° communication strategy for our flour brand's entry into a neighbouring country's retail market. We want to build awareness and trial among households there."

**Interrogation Findings:**
- Q1 (Real business problem): This is a market entry, not just a communication challenge. The real problem is establishing shelf credibility and consumer trust for a brand from a neighbouring country in a market with established local and international flour players.
- Q2 (What's been tried): No prior presence in the market. No brand equity to build on. Starting from zero.
- Q3 (Real audience): Local home cooks — primarily women 30-55 who are the primary household purchasers of staple goods. Their decision driver isn't awareness — it's trust in consistency and origin.
- Q4 (Belief to shift): From "a brand I don't know" to "a brand that understands what I'm making and has been trusted by kitchens across the region for decades."
- Q5 (The one thing): Earn the right to be in the local kitchen — not just on the local shelf.
- Q6 (Brand permission): The brand's regional heritage is the permission — its home market's culinary culture shares significant DNA with the new market's. The brand is not foreign; it is neighboring.
- Q7 (Category gap): Most flour brands in the market communicate on functional benefits (protein content, texture, value). No brand is communicating on culinary heritage and emotional trust.
- Q8 (Risk): Foreign origin could read as "not one of us" if handled wrong.

**Reframed Brief:**
*ORIGINAL REQUEST:* Build awareness and trial in the new market.
*REAL PROBLEM:* Earn enough trust to belong in the local kitchen — not just the shopping basket.
*REAL AUDIENCE:* Local home cooks who make significant decisions about staple brands based on reliability and inherited trust, not promotion.
*BELIEF TO SHIFT:* From: "a brand I've never heard of" → To: "a brand that kitchens across the region have trusted for years — and now it's here."
*THE ONE THING:* Establish the brand as the flour with regional culinary roots — not a newcomer, a neighbor arriving.
*BRAND'S RIGHT TO SAY IT:* Decades of regional presence. Culinary traditions shared across borders.
*OPEN QUESTIONS:* What does the brand's distribution footprint look like at launch? (This determines whether brand-building or trial-driving should lead.)
*REFRAMED BRIEF IN ONE SENTENCE:* Introduce the brand to local kitchens not as a new brand to try, but as a regional brand that finally arrived.

## Example 2 — QSR Chain Multi-Market App Campaign

**Original Request:**
"Media buying for a QSR chain's app awareness campaign across several markets. Youthful tone, a short flight."

**Interrogation Findings:**
- Q1 (Real business problem): App adoption is the metric, but the business problem is likely repeat purchase and order frequency — the app is the mechanism for that, not the end goal.
- Q2 (What's been tried): Not stated. Key question: what is the current app download rate and where does the drop-off happen? Is the problem awareness, download intent, or post-download engagement?
- Q3 (Real audience): Youth 18-28, mobile-first, already using food delivery apps habitually. The question is: are they aware of this brand's app specifically, or are they choosing competitors' apps out of habit?
- Q4 (Belief to shift): From "I order from them when I'm already there" → To "I open their app when I'm deciding what to order."
- Q5 (The one thing): Make the app the first tap when hunger arrives — not the restaurant the first thought.
- Q6 (Brand permission): The brand's youthful positioning and regional presence gives permission for mobile-native, fast-paced communication.
- Q7 (Category gap): Most QSR app campaigns focus on discounts and promotions. Few own the moment of decision — the ten seconds before someone opens a delivery app.
- Q8 (Risk): A short flight is too short for behavior change. Needs a strong download incentive baked into the creative, not just media.

**Reframed Brief in One Sentence:** *Make the brand's app the first tap when young people open their phone hungry — own the decision moment, not just the download.*

## Example 3 — Private Hospital Specialist Surgery Campaign

**Original Request:**
"Awareness and lead generation campaign for a private hospital's specialist surgical department. Target: qualified leads. Emphasize the international clinical affiliation and the consultants' credentials."

**Interrogation Findings:**
- Q1 (Real business problem): Underutilization of a high-investment specialty department. The hospital has the clinical capability; patients are not choosing the hospital for this kind of surgery.
- Q3 (Real audience): Two audiences with different jobs: (a) patients or family members researching specialists after a diagnosis — in a state of anxiety and urgency; (b) referring physicians who decide where to send complex cases.
- Q4 (Belief to shift): For patients: From "it's a good general hospital" → To "it's where I go for something this serious." For referring physicians: From "I refer complex cases to [established competitor]" → To "this surgical team is the credible peer referral."
- Q5 (The one thing): Establish clinical credibility, not just awareness. A patient who becomes aware of the department but doesn't trust it converts into nothing.
- Q6 (Brand permission): The international clinical affiliation is the unlock — it provides a credibility shortcut that few local hospitals can claim.
- Q8 (Risk): Clinical credibility cannot be claimed — it must be demonstrated. Lead-generation tactics that don't build trust first will generate unqualified leads.

**Reframed Brief in One Sentence:** *Make the hospital the name that surfaces when a family faces a serious diagnosis — through credibility first, awareness second.*

## Example 4 — New Business Pitch ({{AGENCY_NAME}} Self-Promotion)

**Original Request:**
"We need to build our credentials deck for pitching to mid-to-large brands in {{MARKETS}} in the B2B and B2C space."

**Interrogation Findings:**
- Q1 (Real problem): A credentials deck is a sales tool — but the real problem it's solving is often unclear. Is the agency losing pitches it should be winning? Not getting invited to pitches? Winning the wrong clients? Each requires a different credentials strategy.
- Q3 (Real audience): The decision-makers reviewing credentials are typically CMOs or marketing directors who have seen hundreds of decks. The question isn't "are we credible?" — it's "are we interesting enough to invite to the next conversation?"
- Q4 (Belief to shift): From "another regional agency" → To "the agency that thinks about our market in a way no one else does."
- Q5 (The one thing): The credentials deck should not prove capability — it should demonstrate point of view. Capability is table stakes. POV is the differentiator.
- Q7 (Category gap): Most agency credentials decks look identical: case studies, client logos, team photos, methodology slides. The white space is a deck that reads like a piece of strategy — that shows, not tells.

**Reframed Brief in One Sentence:** *Build a credentials deck that demonstrates how we think, not just what we've done — because the client is buying the thinking.*

## Example 5 — Regional Brand 12-Month Communication Strategy

**Original Request:**
"Develop a 12-month communication strategy for the brand, grounded in brand audit and social listening insights."

**Interrogation Findings:**
- Q1 (Real problem): A 12-month strategy is a planning horizon, not a problem definition. The real question is: what is the brand trying to achieve in 12 months that it hasn't achieved before?
- Q2 (What's been tried): Social listening insights exist — which means there is existing data on what audiences are saying and feeling. The strategy should start from what that data reveals, not from a blank brief.
- Q4 (Belief to shift): Needs to be determined from the brand audit. What is the current brand perception? What is the gap between current perception and desired perception?
- Q5 (The one thing): 12-month strategies that try to do everything produce 12 months of mediocre communication. The interrogation must force a single strategic priority — the one shift that, if achieved, makes everything else possible.
- Q8 (Risk): A communications strategy that isn't grounded in a clear brand positioning will produce well-executed incoherence — a year of content that doesn't add up to anything.

**Open Questions Before Strategy Begins:**
- What does the brand audit reveal about current perception vs. desired perception?
- What does social listening show — what are people saying, and what are they not saying?
- What is the one commercial priority for the brand in the next 12 months?
- Who is the primary audience — and what do they believe about the brand today?

**Reframed Brief in One Sentence:** *Before building 12 months of communication, answer one question: what does the brand want to mean to its audience that it doesn't mean today?*
